\section{Retrieval error and the transmission profile}\label{sec:results-transfer}

The transfer theorem identifies the ratio of component error to transmission as a
retrieval diagnostic. The conditional-profile theorem additionally requires a bound on
$\E[e_R^2\mid t]$ for a population and, for rate statements, an appropriate sequence of
errors. These are distinct uses of the theory. This section describes the archived
finite-table diagnostics without interpreting a fitted log--log slope as an asymptotic
learning rate.

Table~\ref{tab:v2-transmission} uses the four common float64 prediction records at seeds
104--107. Each contains the same nine heads on 2{,}331 test states and 285 bands, or
664{,}335 state--band entries. The separate seed-101 record contains only three heads,
uses float32 predictions and explicitly opts into precision-altered scoring. It is not
pooled into this comparison. The revised four-record means are a reanalysis of existing
summaries, not a new training campaign. The retrieval uses $\rho=0.7$, $R=0.9$ and a
training-derived albedo bound $S$.

\paragraph{Physical domain and numerical scope.}
The driver defines $S$ as the largest finite training albedo not exceeding one. The
records count 227 or 454 training entries above one, depending on the split. On seed
104, 228 of the 664{,}335 test \emph{entries}, not 228 of the 2{,}331 states, exceed $S$;
the other three records report no exceedance. There are also 203, 6, 3 and 3
nonpositive-transmission entries on seeds 104, 105, 106 and 107, respectively.
The stored pointwise check restricts to $s\le S$ but does not enforce the full
hypothesis $t>0$, $0\le s\le S$, nor does it recompute the forward/inverse pair in
arbitrary precision. Its zero reported violations are therefore implementation
diagnostics, not a verification of every premise of Theorem~\ref{thm:transfer}.
The reported expectations and MSE ratios are full-table averages. Recomputing all of
them on a common fully admissible domain requires the sample-level arrays, which are
not public.

\paragraph{Observed error profiles.}
On the common cohort, the feature kernel has $\varepsilon_R=0.0514$ and the single
network $0.1650$. Mean fitted profile slopes $2\nu$ range from $0.16$ to $0.28$.
The raw 95th-percentile error is $0.075$ for the single-member feature kernel and
$0.060$ for concatenated features, compared with $0.143$ for the campaign stack.
Their constrained 95th percentiles are $0.076$, $0.061$ and $0.151$.
The diagnostic middle-bound/MSE ratios range from about $3.4$ to $5.3$ across heads;
this is an observed numerical comparison, not a guarantee on the full-table distribution.

About five percent of entries have transmission below $3\times10^{-4}$ of the training
flux scale. Among positive transmissions, the one-percent quantile is of order
$10^{-10}$ of that scale. The recorded share of squared retrieval-weighted component
error below $0.03$ of the scale is less than a thousandth for each head. Together,
these measurements are consistent with amplification of small absolute errors by weak
transmission. They do not make the heads irrelevant to the inverse tail: the location,
sign and dependence of their errors still matter, as the exact identities show.

The finite fitted slopes establish neither a population tail law down to zero nor a
conditional-error envelope uniform over training sizes. In particular, they do not
establish that the worst-case exponent in Theorem~\ref{thm:transfer}(iii) is attained by
these emulators. On a prespecified admissible passband $t\ge t_0>0$, the deterministic
bound in item (iv) applies directly. Choosing that passband changes the evaluation
domain and must be accompanied by coverage and failure counts; it is not a way to
replace the all-band diagnostic with an unreported easier task.

The concatenated-feature kernel has mean radiance error $0.0688\%$, compared with

In this retained aggregate, the mean-relative-norm stack has radiance error $0.0754\%$
and constrained 95th-percentile error $0.141$, while the row-relative squared-error

\paragraph{Kernel choice on the state and on learned features.}
Table~\ref{tab:v2-multikernel} is regenerated from twelve public component/seed records,
covering four components and seeds 101--103. The spectral mixture and Mat\'ern-5/2 have
similar mean errors on $Y_1$ ($0.089\%$). The spectral mixture has lower mean errors on
$Y_2$ and $Y_3$ ($0.237\%$ and $0.099\%$) than the other tested raw-state kernels.
The tested first-order additive kernel has much larger errors; this does not prove
that the underlying simulator components cannot be approximated by an additive model
under a different specification. Albedo errors for the nonadditive kernels remain
between roughly $2.3\%$ and $3.5\%$.

\paragraph{Network regularization under the tested budget.}
Table~\ref{tab:v2-regsuite} uses three public seed records for a control, a weight-decay
grid and an early-stopping grid. The validation-selected weight decay gives a similar
standalone component mean to the control ($2.27\%$); the selected early-stopping
configuration is less accurate and more variable ($2.72\pm0.45\%$). Residual correction
reduces the component means to $1.88$--$1.95\%$, and feature kernels to
$0.95$--$0.99\%$, with radiance errors near $0.15\%$. The input reference here is
\emph{isotropic}, not the coordinate-searched input-scaled kernel of the primary table.
These results do not establish that regularization is generally ineffective, only
that the tested changes do not produce comparable gains under this budget.

\paragraph{Perturbation and ridge-path diagnostics.}
The extended tables in Appendix~\ref{app:diagnostics} keep all perturbation rows at a
readable size. Feature-perturbation rows are regenerated from eight public records;
the fourteen state-input rows are retained from their historical table because their
JSON is absent. The residual-action bound is often substantially smaller than the
spectrum-only bound. Nevertheless, its ratio to actual movement can be tens, thousands
or much larger. Positive definiteness can make a bound applicable without making it a
useful application tolerance. The reported feature-record identity and bound checks
are numerical checks on fitted systems, not generalization tests.

Table~\ref{tab:v2-ridge} reports a 4{,}000-row fit and four components at seed 101.
At the smallest ridge on input-space $Y_1$, the effective degrees of freedom are
3{,}841 and the noise factor is $0.924$. The same-half and split-half PCA errors are
$0.079\%$ and $2.465\%$, whereas the physical-unit test errors for the corrected and
standalone kernels are $0.2168\%$ and $0.1754\%$. These columns use different metrics
and evaluation samples and should not be subtracted as like-for-like gains. At
$\gamma=10^{-2}$ the corresponding physical-unit test errors are $0.5095\%$ and
$5.7578\%$: the fitted mean retains predictive information when the kernel correction
is strongly regularized. The ridge path is descriptive; Proposition~\ref{prop:sensitivity}
does not guarantee this test-error ordering.

\section{Synthetic reference for the transfer theorem}\label{sec:results-synthetic}

Table~\ref{tab:v2-synthetic} is a manufactured-data check, not additional evidence about
EMIT. The revision reruns the existing construction with two million samples per
$\beta$, seed zero, at $\beta=0.5,1,2,3,4$, and archives the resulting JSON.
The average-error slopes are $0.200$, $0.333$, $0.498$, $0.597$ and $0.664$, compared
with theoretical limits $0.200$, $0.333$, $0.500$, $0.600$ and $0.667$.
The finite-ladder uniform-family slopes are compared with the analytic curve on the
\emph{same} ladder, rather than only its limiting exponent. At $\beta=2$, the measured
slope is $1.721$ and the analytic finite-ladder slope $1.724$; the limit is two.

The largest relative Monte Carlo discrepancy is about $1.01\%$ for the uniform family
at $\beta=2$ and below $0.053\%$ for the profile family against its numerical-quadrature
reference. The rare-event counts determine which average-family points enter each
slope fit. No pointwise violations are found across the tested predictions on the ten
million sampled transmissions. This numerical agreement supplements, but does not
replace, the exact proofs.

\section{The matched benchmark and the transfer contrast}\label{sec:results-benchmarks}

\paragraph{Correction-coefficient benchmark.}
Table~\ref{tab:v2-pkanrtm} compares archived scores under the standard and
out-of-distribution protocols of \cite{pkan}, with and without low-fidelity 6S
coefficients as inputs. The published reference is not retrained here, and the
comparison does not control for all differences in optimization or computational
budget. The claim is therefore a comparison with the published values under the
stated protocol, not an experimentally isolated architectural advantage.

The state-only residual-corrected network gives standard-split RMSE
$0.00581\pm0.00004$, MAE $0.00151$, uniform-average $R^2=0.9952$ and SMAPE $3.54\%$,
compared with published values $0.00619$, $0.00186$, $0.9947$ and $3.91\%$.
With 6S inputs its means are $0.00578$, $0.00148$, $0.9954$ and $3.19\%$.
On the specified out-of-distribution split, the state-only means are $0.00620$,
$0.00252$, $0.9967$ and $3.37\%$, compared with published values $0.01022$,
$0.00544$, $0.9942$ and $5.40\%$. With 6S inputs the means become $0.00596$,
$0.00232$, $0.9969$ and $2.92\%$. The spreads describe three seeds on the standard
protocol and three initializations on the out-of-distribution protocol.

The $R^2$ convention is material. The reference comparison weights the three
coefficients equally. The released rescore records instead store a variance-weighted
$R^2$ alongside coefficientwise RMSE. The table generator reconstructs the common
per-coefficient target scales from the recorded mean normalized RMSE across heads,
checks them against the stored variance-weighted $R^2$, and computes the
uniform-average value. This reconstruction uses archived summary identities rather
than rescoring public raw predictions. It now fails explicitly when that convention
cannot be reconstructed, rather than silently substituting the other $R^2$.
The ensemble without correction is close to the corrected network, while the raw-state
kernel is much less accurate in these recorded configurations. These results should
be read alongside the input, approximation and tuning choices, not as a general
ordering of kernels and networks.

\paragraph{OCO-2 coefficient and radiance objectives.}
Table~\ref{tab:v2-oco2} is regenerated from nine public selection records: three bands
and seeds 0--2. The feature kernel trained under the flat coefficient loss gives
coefficient errors of approximately $4.1\%$, $16.3\%$ and $8.1\%$ on O$_2$, weak
CO$_2$ and strong CO$_2$, but radiance errors of $0.076\%$, $0.114\%$ and $0.108\%$.
A radiance-weighted feature construction instead gives approximately $0.031\%$,
$0.048\%$ and $0.064\%$ in radiance, with larger coefficient errors. Thus the
reconstruction map and evaluation metric can change which fitted representation is
preferred.

The radiance stack minimizes a \emph{row-relative squared} radiance loss, a convex
quadratic because the reconstruction is affine. The reported radiance error is the
mean of row-relative norms, not that squared fitting objective. The recorded solver
has KKT gaps from $4.17\times10^{-6}$ to $1.52\times10^{-5}$ for its quadratic program; this does not certify
optimality in the reported norm or on test data. Its mean radiance errors are
$0.0264\%$, $0.0391\%$ and $0.0511\%$, compared with $0.0291\%$, $0.0404\%$ and
$0.0536\%$ for the reduced-coordinate squared-loss stack. Coefficient errors increase
to $18.59\%$ and $37.43\%$ on O$_2$ and weak CO$_2$, and to $8.87\%$ on strong
CO$_2$. All rules use 2{,}000 validation and 2{,}000 test rows in these records.
The ten-split companion comparisons in the following section are a separate campaign,
not ten repetitions of this three-seed selection experiment.
