\begin{abstract}
We compare neural networks, exact Mat\'ern regression and their combinations on a
six-dimensional radiative-transfer table over the 285-band EMIT grid. Across ten partitions
with 18{,}884 training samples and validation-based selection, an input-scaled kernel gives
$0.095\%$ mean relative radiance error, compared with $0.760\%$ for cubic regression and
$0.387\%$ for a neural network. Residual correction reduces the network error to $0.141\%$;
a kernel on wider-network features reaches $0.079\%$. The wide convex stack improves the
mean radiance error by only $0.0006$ percentage points while increasing the mean within-split
95th-percentile reflectance error from $7.57$ to $22.71$ points, with a larger tail at every
split. For a physically constrained retrieval, we prove a constant-one bound by the
transmission-weighted component error divided by the transmission. We give a sharp
worst-case transfer exponent under a transmission-tail hypothesis and conditional-error
conditions that retain the component-error rate. We also distinguish the raw-error quadratic
used to fit retrieval-weighted stacks from the projected, nonlinear retrieval used to score
them. The experiments concern interpolation of one adaptively explored table: overlapping
partitions and finite-range transmission profiles do not establish independent validation
or asymptotic learning rates. Supplementary OCO-2 and multi-fidelity comparisons illustrate
how the evaluation metric and input representation change the relative performance of the
specified model families.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Atmospheric correction for imaging spectroscopy uses radiative-transfer calculations to
relate atmospheric and viewing conditions to measured radiance. Learned emulators replace
repeated simulator evaluations with a fitted map to spectral coefficients. Neural
emulators have been incorporated into reflectance-retrieval systems \cite{srtmnet,isofit};
Gaussian-process emulators have also been developed for the OCO-2 forward model
\cite{lamminpaa}. Physics-guided multi-fidelity models use lower-fidelity calculations as
additional information \cite{pkan}. These approaches motivate two distinct questions:
which representation gives an accurate forward emulator, and which errors matter when
that emulator is used in an inverse calculation?

We study direct kernel regression, neural networks and two post-hoc combinations. In
residual correction, the network supplies a mean and a kernel fits its training residual.
In feature regression, the network supplies a frozen representation and a kernel fits the
target on that representation \cite{nmkc}. These constructions differ from jointly trained
deep kernel learning \cite{wilson} and from regularizing intermediate network layers with
kernel flows \cite{yoo}. Their comparison complements work on kernel competitors for
operator learning \cite{batlle}, neural representations such as DeepONet and Fourier
neural operators \cite{deeponet,fno}, and classical kernel regression \cite{rw}.

The principal experiment uses 23{,}313 tabulated radiative-transfer evaluations on the
285-band EMIT wavelength grid \cite{green}. The input has six coordinates and the
standardized outputs have a small principal-component representation, making a dense
kernel solve feasible at the full training size. Ten common partitions compare the model
families. Additional experiments vary training size, output rank, input scaling, network
width and kernel selection. The input-scaled kernel is substantially more accurate than
the isotropic kernel. A wider network has a larger standalone error under the tested
training configuration but supplies features on which a kernel predicts more accurately.
These findings concern the fitted implementations and budgets, not an intrinsic ordering
of kernels and networks.

The central empirical result is a forward--inverse reversal. The wide stack's mean
radiance improvement over its feature kernel is $0.0006$ percentage points, whereas its
mean within-split 95th-percentile reflectance error rises from $7.57$ to $22.71$ points.
The reflectance tail increases at all ten partitions. These are all-band diagnostics at
surface reflectance $0.7$, including poorly conditioned absorption entries; they are not
estimates of operational retrieval accuracy. The result shows that a small forward gain
can accompany a substantial change in an inverse-error tail.

Our analysis separates three levels of explanation. Exact finite-perturbation identities
relate radiance and reflectance errors through the inverse denominator. A constant-one
transfer inequality controls a physically constrained retrieval by component error relative
to transmission. Population tail and conditional-error assumptions then yield rate bounds,
including a sharp worst-case exponent for this inverse. The transmission profiles measured
on a finite table are diagnostics of the error-amplification mechanism, not verification of
those assumptions arbitrarily close to zero. For residual correction, a separate
Hilbert-space identity gives the exact error-alignment criterion, while a PCA decomposition
keeps truncation error distinct from kernel-regression error.

The stacking analysis is implementation-specific. We distinguish a total-flux quadratic
from the separable direct- and diffuse-flux quadratic used by the diagnostic driver.
Projection is applied to the summed flux during retrieval scoring, not inside the fitted
quadratic. The latter therefore remains convex, but the numerical weighting routine is an
approximation to simplex-constrained least squares rather than a certified exact optimizer.
The retrieval bound holds for any feasible weights on its stated physical domain; it does
not assert that minimizing a validation surrogate improves every test metric.

\paragraph{Scope of the evidence.}
The main comparisons have complete per-split summaries, but model development included
inspection of test results from this same table. The overlapping partitions quantify
within-table split sensitivity rather than ten independent confirmation experiments.
Secondary evidence has unequal replication and archival completeness. We distinguish
recomputable summaries, retained aggregate tables and experiments requiring unavailable
sample-level arrays. The published benchmark comparison uses the benchmark's stated
metrics and archived scores; it is not a retraining of its reference model under a matched
computational budget.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Radiative transfer and its surrogates.}
Relevant radiative-transfer codes include 6S \cite{vermote}, libRadtran \cite{mayer,emde}
and MODTRAN \cite{modtran}; lookup-table calculations enter atmospheric correction
\cite{isofit} and optimal estimation \cite{rodgers}. These references describe the surrounding
methodology, not a recovered generation configuration for the EMIT arrays used here.
The carbon-dioxide product of
OCO-2 \cite{eldering,crisp} rests on the forward model and inverse method of \cite{connor,odell}.
Surrogates enter because the forward model is the cost. Neural emulation of longwave radiation
goes back to \cite{chevallier} and was carried into climate models by \cite{krasnopolsky}; later
work measures how accurate such emulators can be made \cite{veerman,ukkonen} and how learned
parameterizations behave when the host model is stepped forward \cite{rasp,brenowitz}. In imaging
spectroscopy and land-surface inference the same idea appears as emulator toolboxes for
radiative-transfer models \cite{rivera,verrelst}, Gaussian-process emulation for parameter
retrieval \cite{gomezdans}, and the wider use of Gaussian processes in earth observation
\cite{campsvalls}; \cite{srtmnet} places an emulator inside a reflectance retrieval and
\cite{lamminpaa} fits a cross-validated Gaussian process to the OCO-2 forward model. Replacing an
expensive simulator by a statistical model is a classical problem \cite{sacks}, as is using a
cheap model to inform an expensive one \cite{kennedyohagan,kennedycalib,perdikaris}, surveyed for
propagation and inference in \cite{peherstorfer}; the network of \cite{pkan} is a multi-fidelity
instance on the corpus rescored in Section~\ref{sec:results-benchmarks}. The measurement
requirements behind the tables used here are those of the EMIT mission \cite{green} and of the
surface-imaging missions that follow it \cite{cawse}.

\paragraph{Kernel regression.}
The estimators are classical: interpolation in a reproducing-kernel Hilbert space
\cite{aronszajn}, smoothing splines and the representer theorem
\cite{kimeldorf,wahba,representer}, ridge regularization \cite{hoerl} read as spectral filtering
of an ill-posed problem \cite{engl}, and the Mat\'ern family, whose smoothness index fixes the
differentiability of the sample paths \cite{stein,rw}. Rates under source and capacity conditions
are given in \cite{cdv,sutherland,fs,smalezhou}; the textbook accounts are
\cite{scholkopf,steinwart,esl}. Each component here is a vector of principal coefficients, so the
multi-output formulation is the relevant one \cite{micchelli,alvarez}. Kernels also serve as
solvers rather than regressors: Bayesian numerical homogenization \cite{owhadibnh},
operator-adapted wavelets \cite{owhadiscovel} and the Gaussian-process treatment of nonlinear
partial differential equations \cite{chenowhadi} use the same estimator, and cross-validating the
kernel itself gives the kernel-flow family \cite{owhadiyoo,hamziowhadi,yoo}.

\paragraph{Cost.}
An exact solve is cubic in the number of training rows, which has produced a long line of
approximations: the Nystr\"om method and its analysis \cite{nystrom,drineas,bachlowrank}, random
features \cite{rahimirecht} and their operator-learning counterpart \cite{nelsenstuart},
preconditioned solvers at large scale \cite{falkon,meanti}, inducing-point and variational
Gaussian processes \cite{quinonero,titsias,hensman}, and structured interpolation with
hardware-aware linear algebra \cite{kissgp,gpytorch}. At $n = 18{,}884$ the exact factorization is
affordable, so approximation enters this study only as a perturbation whose size is measured
(Section~\ref{sec:results-transfer}).

\paragraph{Choosing and combining kernels.}
A kernel can be built rather than picked: spectral mixtures \cite{wilsonadams}, additive structure
\cite{additivegp}, compositional search over a grammar \cite{duvenaudstructure}, and
per-coordinate relevance fitted by marginal likelihood \cite{rw} or by cross-validation
\cite{owhadiyoo}. Several kernels can be combined instead of one being chosen, by semidefinite
programming \cite{lanckriet}, by the algorithms surveyed in \cite{gonen}, or by alignment with the
target Gram matrix \cite{cristianini,cortesalign}. Combining predictors rather than kernels is
older still \cite{bates-granger,wolpert,breiman}. Within each run, selection uses validation rows \cite{stonecv,gcv,arlot}.
Section~\ref{sec:damped} gives a finite-class guarantee under a stronger independent-selection
protocol, which the reused validation sets in this study do not satisfy.

\paragraph{Networks, and the kernels they induce.}
Feedforward networks are universal approximators \cite{hornik}; those trained here use the
standard adaptive optimizer \cite{adam} with decoupled weight decay \cite{adamw} and early
stopping \cite{prechelt}. Dropout is another established regularizer \cite{dropout},
but is not an arm of the reported regularization table. In the
infinite-width limit a network is a Gaussian process \cite{neal,leenngp,matthews} whose kernel is
the arc-cosine kernel of \cite{chosaul}, and gradient descent on a wide network follows the neural
tangent kernel \cite{ntk,arora}; both enter the kernel comparison of
Section~\ref{sec:results-representation} as heads. The limit is not the whole story, since a
finite network learns its representation \cite{rfm}, and the comparison between kernel machines
and trained networks is itself unsettled \cite{belkin}; a learned representation under a kernel is
what deep kernel learning \cite{wilson}, the Gaussian process with a neural-operator mean
\cite{mora} and the construction of \cite{nmkc} exploit. Kolmogorov--Arnold networks
\cite{kolmogorov,kan} are the architecture behind the multi-fidelity model of \cite{pkan}.
Operator learning supplies the other comparison: DeepONet \cite{deeponet}, Fourier neural
operators \cite{fno} and the general formulation \cite{kovachki}, with fair-comparison protocols
\cite{lufair}, physics-informed training \cite{pinn}, the cost-accuracy trade-off \cite{dehoop},
kernel competitors \cite{batlle}, and the benchmark collections \cite{pdebench,thewell,climsim}
from which Section~\ref{sec:corpora} draws.

\section{Data and protocol}\label{sec:data}

The data are $n = 23{,}313$ tabulated radiative-transfer evaluations. Each
input $x \in \mathbb{R}^6$ collects the atmospheric and viewing state, over the ranges on
which such lookup tables are built for imaging-spectroscopy atmospheric correction
\cite{isofit,modtran}. Read from the archive, the sampled extrema are an aerosol optical
depth in $[0.0500, 1.2000]$, a surface elevation in $[-0.4997, 5.9998]$ km, a water-vapour
column in $[0.0502, 6.9999]$ cm, a relative azimuth in $[0.0029, 179.9947]^\circ$, a solar
zenith angle in $[0.0012, 64.9981]^\circ$, and a view zenith angle in
$[150.0003, 179.9991]^\circ$ from the downward vertical, that is within $30^\circ$ of nadir.
Each output is a set
of four spectral components on the same 285-point EMIT wavelength grid spanning
381--2493\,nm: path radiance $Y_1$, direct flux $Y_2$, diffuse flux $Y_3$, and spherical albedo
$Y_4$ (Figure~\ref{fig:examples}). The quantities retrieved downstream are the radiance at a
test surface reflectance $\rho$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fwd}
L(\rho) \;=\; Y_1 + \rho\,\frac{Y_2 + Y_3}{1 - \rho\, Y_4},
\end{equation}
and the reflectance recovered by inverting \eqref{eq:fwd} with predicted components at the true
radiance,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inv}
\hat\rho \;=\; \frac{L - \hat Y_1}{\hat Y_2 + \hat Y_3 + \hat Y_4 (L - \hat Y_1)},
\end{equation}
both evaluated at $\rho = 0.7$ in the primary comparison. The archive carries the table but
no record of the run that produced it, so the simulator version and generation
configuration are not established; the identity of the archive, and what it does and does
not contain, are set out in the data statement.

The protocol is the same for every family. The table is split into a test block of 2{,}331
rows and a remaining block of 20{,}982; 2{,}098 of the latter are held out for validation,
leaving 18{,}884 for fitting. The procedure is repeated with seeds 101--110. Standardization
and principal components are fitted only on each split's training rows. Hyperparameters,
checkpoints, coordinatewise selections and combination weights use that split's validation
rows. Each fitted family is then evaluated on that split's test rows. Unless stated otherwise,
tables report the mean and sample standard deviation over the ten splits.

The model families and ablations were chosen during exploratory analysis of the same table,
including inspection of test-set results. Repeated partitions therefore measure sensitivity
to the split within this table, rather than independent external validation. Their test
sets overlap, and their standard deviations are descriptive, not confidence intervals from
ten independent datasets. Section~\ref{sec:paired-tails} reports paired differences on
common splits. Validation rows are shared by early stopping, tuning and model combination.
The primary model comparison uses $\rho=0.7$. Supplementary stored retrieval diagnostics
also evaluate a prescribed reflectance grid, but no EMIT atmospheric states outside the
table or independent operational retrievals are tested.

Three empirical structural diagnostics motivate the model comparison.

\emph{The outputs are low rank.} On standardized training outputs, $99.99\%$ of the variance of
every component lies in the first 11--25 principal components, and $99.9999\%$ in at most 44
(Figure~\ref{fig:structure}, left); the minimum adjacent-band correlation across the four
components is $0.994952$ on the training rows. All regressors below
act on the first 64 principal coefficients, whose training unexplained-variance fraction is
below $10^{-6}$, except the networks, which are trained directly on the 285 standardized
bands. A small aggregate variance tail is not a uniform relative-error guarantee in physical
units; Section~\ref{sec:identity} states the projection error explicitly.

\emph{The measured input relevance is anisotropic.} Shuffling the aerosol coordinate reduces
a linear model's validation $R^2$ by $0.89$; the corresponding reduction is $0.54$ for solar
zenith and approximately $0.1$ for elevation and water vapor. The two remaining angles have
smaller effects (Figure~\ref{fig:structure}, right). These validation diagnostics motivate
input-specific kernel scales. They measure sensitivity of the fitted linear model, rather
than identifying an intrinsic dimension of the regression problem.

\emph{Low-degree surrogates are accurate.} Linear regression on the standardized state
achieves $R^2=0.83$, compared with $0.74$ for distance-weighted five-nearest-neighbors.
Cubic polynomial ridge regression has mean relative component error of approximately two
percent (Table~\ref{tab:results}). Together with the spectral compression, these results
motivate smooth low-dimensional regression as a baseline.

Reflectance inversion requires additional care. In deep absorption windows, the transmitted
flux $Y_2+Y_3$ can be as small as $10^{-21}$. At a fully opaque band, the radiance is
independent of surface reflectance and the inverse is non-identifiable. An auxiliary
round-trip calculation on 2{,}332 states gives reflectance RMSE $0.0054$ even with exact
tabulated components. Its error is concentrated in 1{,}652 of 664{,}620 sample--band entries,
whose inverse denominators are below $2\times10^{-13}$. All returned inverses are finite.
This is a floating-point conditioning diagnostic, not a lower bound on emulator error.
We report both the center and the tails of the reflectance-error distribution. Non-finite
inverses are assigned zero by the scoring routine; their frequency is recorded separately.

Differentiating \eqref{eq:inv} separates the components. Write $D = \hat Y_2 + \hat Y_3 +
\hat Y_4 (L - \hat Y_1)$ for its denominator. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sens}
\frac{\partial\hat\rho}{\partial \hat Y_4} = -\hat\rho^{\,2},
\qquad
\frac{\partial\hat\rho}{\partial \hat Y_2} = \frac{\partial\hat\rho}{\partial \hat Y_3}
 = -\frac{\hat\rho}{D},
\qquad
\frac{\partial\hat\rho}{\partial \hat Y_1} = -\frac{\hat Y_2 + \hat Y_3}{D^{2}} .
\end{equation}
At exact identifiable components, $\widehat\rho=\rho=0.7$, so the albedo derivative is
$-0.49$, independently of the positive transmitted flux. For general predictions it equals
$-\widehat\rho^2$ and can be large. The denominator factors in \eqref{eq:sens} make
path-radiance and flux errors particularly important near opaque bands.
Section~\ref{sec:paired-tails} reports the resulting all-band error tails.

\section{Models}\label{sec:methods}

All models are trained per component and evaluated in physical units after undoing the
standardization; unless stated otherwise they regress the 64 leading principal coefficients of
the standardized component, with the training variance criterion and projection
qualification of Section~\ref{sec:data}. Every hyperparameter named below is selected on the validation set
of the seed at hand. The comparisons of Sections~\ref{sec:results}--\ref{sec:retune} run on CPU; the
ten-kernel comparison, the regularisation suite and the matched benchmark of
Sections~\ref{sec:results-representation}--\ref{sec:results-benchmarks} run on one T4 GPU, whose device and
peak memory each run record carries.

\paragraph{Reference family.} Cubic polynomial ridge regression \cite{hoerl}, acting on all monomials of
degree at most three in the six standardized inputs, with the ridge weight selected from
$\{10^{-10},\allowbreak 10^{-8},\allowbreak 10^{-6},\allowbreak 10^{-4},\allowbreak 10^{-2}\}$.
It provides a low-cost, low-degree reference; its empirical error alone does not prove
a smoothness class for the underlying function.

\paragraph{Exact kernel ridge regression.} Three constructions, all Mat\'ern kernels \cite{stein} on the
standardized inputs with the length scale expressed as a multiple of the median pairwise
distance of the fit set. The listed nugget is $\gamma$ in $(K+n\gamma I)\alpha=Z$, not the unscaled
diagonal term. The subset baseline is a Mat\'ern-$5/2$ kernel fitted on a uniformly drawn subset of 4{,}000 training
points --- the landmark choice of a Nystr\"om approximation \cite{nystrom,drineas} --- one
eigendecomposition per length scale, multiplier in $\{1,2\}$, nugget in
$\{10^{-8},10^{-6},10^{-4},10^{-2}\}$. The second is the exact solve on every training row:
smoothness $\nu\in\{3/2,5/2\}$, multiplier in $\{0.5,1,2,4\}$ and the same nugget grid, tuned on
validation from a 6{,}000-row training subsample and the winner refit on all 18{,}884 rows by one
Cholesky factorization \cite{golubvanloan}. The third gives each input its own length scale, an
automatic relevance determination \cite{neal,rw}: starting from the
isotropic winner, a coordinate search over per-input multipliers in $\{1/4,1/2,1,2,4\}$, two
sweeps, scored on validation, after which the smoothness, scale and nugget are re-tuned at the
found metric and the solve is refit on every row. The third construction allows the kernel to adapt to the input anisotropy measured in
Section~\ref{sec:data}.

\paragraph{Fully connected networks.} Three ELU hidden layers of 512 units per component,
trained on the 285 standardized bands with AdamW \cite{adam,adamw} at learning rate $10^{-3}$, cosine decay, batch
size 1024, weight decay $10^{-6}$, up to 150 epochs with early stopping on validation loss \cite{prechelt}
(patience 25). Five networks are trained per component and seed from different initializations;
the table reports the first and the mean of the five.

\paragraph{Kernel corrections of the neural mean.} The construction of \cite{nmkc}, with the
exact kernel stages fitted on every training row. (i) \emph{Residual correction}: an exact Mat\'ern
regression, on the standardized input space, of the network's training residuals in coefficient
space, added back to the network's projection into that space, with smoothness, scale
and nugget selected by the validation error of the corrected prediction; applied to the single network and to the five-network mean.
(ii) \emph{Kernel on features}: the same exact regression with the input metric replaced by the
network's last hidden layer, standardized, giving a kernel metric in the
fitted feature representation. This is the post-hoc counterpart of deep
kernel learning \cite{wilson}, in which the representation and the kernel are trained
together, and of the kernel-flow regularization of a network's inner layers \cite{yoo}; here
the representation is fixed first and the kernel is solved exactly on it. (iii) \emph{Per-coordinate selection}: for
each of the 64 output coefficients independently, pick on validation among the exact kernel, the
network, the ensemble, the two corrected predictors and the feature kernel, and assemble the
test prediction from the winners. (iv) \emph{Convex stack}: per component, a convex combination
of the same six heads with weights fitted on the validation points in the physical relative
metric, in the manner of stacked generalization \cite{wolpert} and stacked regression
\cite{breiman}. The choice of weighting rule for pools of this kind, and its relation to
constrained minimum-variance weighting, is studied separately and is not part of this
comparison.

\paragraph{Sample-size, rank and width experiments.} Section~\ref{sec:scaling} repeats the isotropic
exact kernel, the network, its residual correction and the feature kernel at nested training
sizes, at other output ranks and at a wider network; the table of Section~\ref{sec:results} is
the full-size, rank-64, width-512 cell of that grid.

Table~\ref{tab:results} compares the model families at 18{,}884 training rows, output rank
64 and network width 512.

\paragraph{Polynomial and subset-kernel baselines.}
Cubic ridge regression gives $1.86\%$ mean component error and $0.760\%$ radiance error.
The 4{,}000-point Mat\'ern fit reduces these means to $0.88\%$ and $0.332\%$. Its albedo
error varies substantially across splits: approximately $1.9\%$ on seven splits and
$3.3$--$4.3\%$ on the remaining three.

\paragraph{Full-data and input-scaled kernels.}
The isotropic full-data kernel reduces mean radiance error to $0.239\%$ and path-radiance
error to $0.08\%$, compared with $0.17\%$ for the subset fit. Its albedo error is
$2.3$--$2.9\%$ across splits: less variable, but larger than the subset fit on the latter's
seven most accurate splits. Input-specific length scales give $0.095\%$ radiance error,
$0.63\%$ mean component error and $0.036$ percentage points of median reflectance error.
The fitted scales are consistent with weak azimuth dependence of the flux components. The
relative-azimuth length scale is sixteen to thirty-two times larger than the other scales,
with median factors sixteen for direct flux and albedo and thirty-two for diffuse flux.
The longer azimuth scale occurs on every split for the flux components and nine of ten
for albedo. View-zenith scales are two to sixteen times longer, whereas the path-radiance
metric is approximately isotropic.

\paragraph{Networks and kernel corrections.}
The network has $1.22\%$ mean component error and $0.387\%$ radiance error. Averaging five
initializations reduces these to $0.96\%$ and $0.251\%$. Residual kernel regression reduces
the single-network radiance error to $0.141\%$ and the ensemble error to $0.123\%$.
The feature kernel has $0.61\%$ mean component error and $0.098\%$ radiance error. Its
radiance comparison with the input-scaled kernel is close: the feature kernel is lower on
six of ten splits, although its mean is slightly larger. Its median reflectance error is
$0.054$ points, compared with $0.036$ for the input-scaled kernel, and is larger on every
split. The separately tuned residual correction substantially improves the network, but
has larger radiance error than the input-scaled and feature kernels.

\paragraph{Selection and stacking.}
Both combiners use the isotropic full-data kernel, the network, its five-member ensemble,
the two residual-corrected predictors and the feature kernel. The input-scaled kernel is not
in this pool. Coordinatewise validation selection gives $0.193\%$ radiance error, larger
than several individual members. The convex stack gives $0.087\%$ radiance error,
$0.57\%$ mean component error and $0.048$ points of median reflectance error. Its radiance
gain is approximately $8\%$ relative to the input-scaled kernel and $11\%$ relative to its
best individual member, the feature kernel. On mean component error, the stack improves on
the input-scaled kernel at nine splits, with paired difference $0.06\pm0.05$ points, and on
the feature kernel at all ten, with difference $0.047\pm0.006$ points.

The residual second moments provide a diagnostic of complementarity. On path radiance, the
isotropic kernel and five-network ensemble have residual correlation $0.27\pm0.09$, below
their error ratio of approximately $0.34$ at every split. For a quadratic error metric, this
is the classical condition under which a convex combination can improve on the better
member \cite{bates-granger,nmkc}. The stack instead minimizes a mean relative norm, and its
reflectance objective is nonlinear; the diagnostic does not guarantee improvement in those
metrics. The two residual-corrected predictors have much larger correlations, approximately
$0.88$ on path radiance and $0.99$ on albedo. Coordinatewise selection also differs from
stacking in its objective and number of independently chosen coefficients.

\paragraph{Spherical albedo.}
Albedo has the largest relative error for every family. Its small short-wave-infrared
values make relative errors sensitive to absolute prediction errors in that region. The
width-512 stack achieves $1.94\%$ albedo error. The learning curves below show little
improvement with sample size on this component; the experiments do not isolate whether
the plateau arises from approximation, optimization or the relative-error weighting.

\paragraph{Tuning with every row in the solve.} The kernel families of
Section~\ref{sec:methods} choose smoothness, scale and nugget by the validation error of a solve
on a $6{,}000$-row subsample, and refit the winner on all $18{,}884$ rows. Doing the tuning
solve on every row instead lowers the isotropic kernel's radiance error from $0.239\%$ to
$0.210\%$ and its reflectance median from $0.141$ to $0.107$ points, better at nine of the ten
splits, the ratio averaging $0.882$ and never above $1.001$. The selected scales differ: over the forty component-seed cells the full block picks a shorter length scale than
the subsample in $26$, the same in $12$, and a longer one in $2$. In this experiment, subsample selection usually chooses a longer length scale than
full-data selection, consistent with the smaller tuning fit favoring greater smoothing.

Below $6{,}000$ rows the tuning subsample \emph{is} the whole training block and
the rungs are already full-block tuned; at $8{,}000$ rows it is three quarters of it; only the top
rung is a third. Applying the measured ratio to the top rung of Table~\ref{tab:curves} takes the
kernel's last step on the radiance from $-0.183$ to $-0.334$, against a slope of $-0.305$ over the
five lower rungs: the top-step slope becomes closer to that of the lower rungs. One retuned endpoint
does not establish a power law over or beyond the observed range. On the two flux components the same correction takes the
last step from $-0.187$ and $-0.171$ to $-0.352$ and $-0.358$ against averages of $-0.437$ and
$-0.468$, reducing, but not eliminating, the difference between the last-step and lower-rung slopes. The consequence for
the comparison of Section~\ref{sec:results} is that the kernel's margin over the network at the
full block is wider than Table~\ref{tab:curves} shows: $1.8$ times rather than $1.6$, under this retuned endpoint. No conclusion about unobserved sample sizes follows.

Full-data tuning has little effect on the input-scaled kernel: $0.096\%$ against $0.095\%$, better at
four of the ten splits, ratio $1.005$. Its coordinate search already selects a scale of once the
median distance at the metric it finds, which is what the full-block tune selects too. The input-scaled kernel is therefore less sensitive to the tuning sample size in
these experiments.

\paragraph{Gradient-based metric.} The coordinate search costs a sweep over the six inputs at
five multipliers, twice, per component and per seed. The alternative sets the metric before any
kernel is fitted: the multiplier of coordinate $j$ is the root-mean-square gradient of the fitted
cubic surrogate along $j$ over the training rows, raised to a power $p$, normalized to geometric
mean one and clipped to the search's own range $[1/16, 4]$, so weakly varying coordinates receive longer length scales. The exponent $p$
controls the strength of the rescaling. It costs one
cubic fit and six finite differences.

At $p=1$, radiance error is
$0.159\%$, below the isotropic kernel at every one of the ten splits by a factor $0.669$ on average
and above the searched metric at every one of them by a factor $1.680$. The dependence on $p$ is nonmonotone: $p=1/2$ gives $0.125\%$ and $p=1/4$ gives $0.157\%$, so
the best exponent is interior, and choosing it the way every other hyperparameter in this study is
chosen --- the lowest mean validation error over the four components, per seed, with the test block
untouched --- selects $p=1/2$ at all ten seeds. At that exponent the metric closes $79\%$ of the
distance from the isotropic kernel to the searched one on the radiance and $82\%$ on the
reflectance median, for one cubic fit, six finite differences and a choice among three numbers.

The componentwise errors favor different exponents: $Y_2$ and $Y_3$ are best at $1/2$, the albedo at $1$
($2.18\%$ against $2.27\%$), and the path radiance at $1/4$.  On that component the isotropic kernel already reads $0.084\%$ and the
searched metric $0.085\%$: coordinate search gives little improvement. The proportional metric nonetheless
reads $0.34\%$ there, approximately four times the isotropic error. A possible explanation is that the surrogate's gradient along a
coordinate measures how fast the map moves with it and not how much predictive information it
carries, so gradient magnitude alone need not provide an appropriate kernel metric. Softening
the exponent to $1/4$ takes $Y_1$ back to $0.085\%$, exactly the searched metric's value, at the
cost of $Y_2$ and $Y_3$. The validated gradient metric recovers much of the observed radiance gain with fewer metric
searches. Coordinate search additionally allows independent componentwise adjustments,
whereas the gradient construction selects one exponent for all four components.

\section{Comparison with OCO-2}\label{sec:oco2}

The companion paper \cite{nmkc} applies the same construction to the OCO-2 radiative-transfer
emulation problem of Lamminp\"a\"a et al.~\cite{lamminpaa}: per spectral band (O$_2$, weak
CO$_2$, strong CO$_2$), a reduced atmospheric state of twenty or twenty-four coordinates maps to
forty reduced radiance coefficients, with errors on the standardized coefficients and on the
reconstructed radiance, under the ten-split protocol of Section~\ref{sec:data}. The corresponding
results in \cite{nmkc} provide a comparison at a higher input dimension.

On this higher-dimensional problem, a kernel on the state trails the network by a factor of nine, four and
five on the reduced coefficients: the isotropic Mat\'ern reaches $40.6\%$, $58.6\%$ and $43.4\%$
on the three bands against $4.4\%$, $16.4\%$ and $8.2\%$ for the network. The residual correction adds no material gain in the reported OCO-2 comparisons.
Little alignment with useful kernel directions is a possible explanation, but the public
records do not measure the residual's Gram-eigenvector coefficients. The design identity
in Proposition~\ref{prop:identity} alone cannot establish that explanation on test data. The coordinate search that gives a kernel
on the state its own length scales, a strong raw-input construction on the EMIT table, is not the
strongest there either. Four learned metrics were run at the same ten splits on all three bands:
a kernel-flow metric \cite{owhadiyoo}, a Mahalanobis metric under the same flow, the gradient outer
product of the recursive feature machine \cite{rfm}, and per-input scales fitted by empirical
Bayes. The last is the best of the four at every band, taking the isotropic kernel's $40.6\%$,
$58.6\%$ and $44.3\%$ on the coefficients to $18.7\%$, $25.3\%$ and $16.9\%$, and on O$_2$ its
radiance from $0.233\%$ to $0.150\%$; the two flow metrics agree within half a point of each other
at $26.9\%$, $29.8\%$ and $23.9\%$, the recursive feature machine reads $28.2\%$, $47.9\%$ and
$31.3\%$, and an additive kernel $38.2\%$, $46.9\%$ and $47.7\%$. The isotropic baseline agrees
between the metric and baseline experiments on O$_2$ and weak CO$_2$ to two decimals; on strong
CO$_2$ it reads $44.3\%$ in the metric experiment and $43.4\%$ in the baseline experiment. This band
also has a spread of $\pm3.4$ because two of its ten splits land three points
below the other eight. The learned metric reduces the isotropic error to between two fifths and a
half of itself depending on the band. The feature kernel remains more accurate, with a band-dependent margin: the exact Mat\'ern regression on the network's frozen features reads $4.1\%$, $16.3\%$
and $8.1\%$ at the same splits, so the best state metric is $4.6$ times that on O$_2$ but only
$1.6$ and $2.1$ times it on the two CO$_2$ bands. The empirical-Bayes fit has substantial split variability: on O$_2$ the
empirical-Bayes metric is bimodal across splits, eight of the ten landing near $17.2\%$ and two at
$24.7\%$, so its spread of $\pm3.2$ is thirty times the isotropic kernel's $\pm0.1$ and its mean
is not the value a single split is likely to show. The feature-kernel construction remains useful on both tasks: the exact Mat\'ern
regression on the network's frozen features beats a ridge readout refitted on the same features at
every split, on every band and under every training loss, and a per-coefficient choice among heads
trained under different losses has lower mean error than the release's stored emulator under both reported metrics. In the archived loss campaign it wins the reduced-coefficient comparison at all ten splits on each band and the radiance comparison at ten, nine and ten splits on O$_2$, weak CO$_2$ and strong CO$_2$, respectively. These companion comparisons are not an independent EMIT validation; their tables are in \cite{nmkc}.

The OCO-2 retrieval records overlap the release's test set. The reference's data release carries 976 retrieval records, each with an atmospheric
state, the full-physics monochromatic radiance at that state, its rank-40 reconstruction, and the
prediction of the release's Gaussian-process emulator at the same forty coefficients. Every one of
the 976 reduced states matches a stored test input within $6.67\times10^{-16}$; the records map
to 974 distinct test rows, and the nearest test-row index is the same in all three bands. The
records are therefore not disjoint from the stored test set and cannot serve as an independent
evaluation pool, though they do provide a different target, the full-physics radiance, at those
states. The state reduction was replayed independently from the release's two
dimensional-reduction files and the \texttt{x\_in} and \texttt{geo\_in} fields of all 976
records; the replay code and its digests are archived with the per-run records.

\section{Ten further emulation configurations}\label{sec:corpora}

Table~\ref{tab:corpora} extends the comparison to ten emulation configurations. Each
family uses validation-based hyperparameter selection. The models and training budgets are
specified below; these comparisons assess the selected family implementations rather than
the best task-specific methods available for each corpus.

The corpora are the advection equation with discontinuous initial data from the operator suite
of \cite{dehoop}, which is also the source of the structural-mechanics problem of \cite{nmkc};
Burgers' equation at three viscosities from PDEBench \cite{pdebench}; three one-step maps from
The Well \cite{thewell} --- a two-dimensional turbulent radiative layer, an active-matter
system, and a Helmholtz staircase; the ClimSim atmospheric physics emulation task
\cite{climsim}; and the paired correction-coefficient corpus of the physics-guided
radiative-transfer work of \cite{pkan}, which is a third radiative-transfer emulation problem
and the largest here at 487{,}790 rows. That last corpus is run in both of its configurations:
from the atmospheric state alone, and with the low-fidelity 6S coefficients supplied as
additional inputs, which is a different and easier problem.

The configurations are these. Advection is the 200-point input/output pair of the operator
suite, used at full resolution with no reduction. Burgers is PDEBench's
\texttt{1D\_Burgers\_Sols\_Nu}$\nu$ release, the map from the initial condition to the solution
at $t = 1$ on a grid subsampled by four to 256 cells, the input used raw and the output reduced
to 128 principal coefficients. The three Well datasets are one-step maps of the full
multi-channel state with 256 principal coefficients in and out: the turbulent radiative layer on
its $128 \times 384$ grid (density, pressure, velocity), active matter on $256 \times 256$
(concentration, velocity, and the two tensor fields), and the Helmholtz staircase on its two
pressure channels. ClimSim is the low-resolution release, 124 inputs to 128 tendencies, with
100{,}000 training rows drawn from the 10.1M available and validation and test taken from the
release's own validation and scoring files. The correction-coefficient corpus is the paired
6S/libRadtran Sentinel-2 set \cite{vermote,mayer}, seven state variables and band wavelength mapping to the three
libRadtran coefficients, split by atmospheric state so that all bands of a state travel
together. The inputs are wavelength, solar zenith, view zenith, relative azimuth, aerosol
optical depth, water vapor, ozone and elevation. The multi-fidelity configuration adds the
three 6S coefficients, giving input dimensions eight and eleven, respectively.
For these two configurations the error is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coefficient-floor}
 E_{0.05}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
 \frac{\|Y_i-\widehat Y_i\|_2}{\max\{\|Y_i\|_2,0.05\}}.
\end{equation}
The same denominator floor enters validation selection and the kernel-flow objective.
It limits the weight of small-norm coefficient vectors; these two rows therefore use a
floored relative error rather than an unmodified relative norm.

The families are those of Section~\ref{sec:methods} with two differences forced by scale. The
reference family is a ridge regression linear in whatever inputs the models receive, not the
cubic of Table~\ref{tab:results}: a cubic expansion of a 256-dimensional input is not tractable,
and the linear reference is used consistently for the additional configurations. And the kernel solves are exact only when the training block has at most
20{,}000 rows; above that they use a Nystr\"om approximation \cite{nystrom} on 6{,}000 uniformly drawn
landmarks. That cap binds on ClimSim, on the Helmholtz staircase at 20{,}384 rows, and on the
two correction-coefficient configurations. Those entries are what a capped fit gives; they are
approximate rather than full dense solves. Their errors need not bound those of the
full kernel, since approximation affects both fit and regularization. The convex stack and the
per-coordinate selection are formed from the kernels, the networks, the residual correction and
the feature kernel; the ridge is a reference row and is not one of their members.

What each row averages differs and is stated in the table. Advection, Burgers, ClimSim and the
two correction-coefficient configurations have three to five random splits. The Well's
releases carry their own train, validation and test files, which are used unchanged, so the
three turbulent-radiative-layer runs share one split and differ only in network initialisation;
their spread, of order $0.01$ points, measures initialisation and not sampling. The active-matter
and Helmholtz-staircase rows are single runs.

small --- Burgers at $\nu = 0.1$ --- the exact kernel is at $2.81\%$ against the network's
$20.34\%$, a factor of seven, of the same kind as the five- to six-fold gap between the
per-input kernel and the network on the EMIT table's smooth components; as the viscosity falls
and the solutions roughen the gap closes to $23.40\%$ against $36.65\%$ and then to $31.66\%$
against $39.98\%$. Where the sample is large and the map rough --- ClimSim, and the
correction-coefficient corpus at nearly half a million rows --- the network leads instead,
$25.10\%$ against $27.20\%$ and $3.75\%$ against $62.93\%$, which is the OCO-2 regime. Where the
operator is linear, on advection, the linear ridge is the lowest row and every other family
lands within $0.4$ points of it, consistent with the linear form of the operator.

Learned metrics give particularly large improvements on the correction-coefficient corpus. On the correction-coefficient corpus from the
state alone the isotropic kernel is at $62.93 \pm 0.51$ and a kernel-flow metric \cite{owhadiyoo}
takes it to $3.97 \pm 0.42$, a factor of sixteen in the mean, which brings a kernel on the state
to within six percent of the network on a corpus where the plain kernel was seventeen times
worse. With the low-fidelity coefficients supplied as inputs the same pair reads $15.76$ and
$4.60$, a factor of three: the easier the problem is made for the plain kernel, the less the
learned metric adds. This comparison, like the EMIT and OCO-2 results, shows that the choice of input metric
can substantially change a kernel baseline.

The convex stack is at or near the best member of its candidate pool on each configuration. The convex stack
is the lowest row on eight of the ten configurations and the second
lowest on the other two; the residual-corrected network is in the lowest two on five. Both
exceptions are the linear problems, and on both the row that beats the stack is the ridge, which
the stack does not contain. On advection the ridge is lower by $0.07$ points while the stack, at
$11.36\%$, matches the best member it has --- the isotropic kernel, on which it puts weight
$0.87$ --- to two decimals. On the Helmholtz staircase the ridge reaches $0.004\%$, sixteen
times below the next row of the table and about a hundred times below the weakest member of the
pool, and the stack reaches $0.066\%$, again a shade below its own best member, the
learned-metric kernel at $0.067\%$, on which it puts weight $0.94$. In both cases the stack does
what it is asked to do inside its pool; what it cannot do is recover a family the pool excludes.
The statement this supports is correspondingly narrow: a combination is a good default when no
member of its own pool dominates it, and a combination can only be read against the whole
comparison when the pool contains the families the comparison includes.

The comparisons have several limitations. These rows use one training budget per corpus and one architecture family,
and several of these corpora have published results from methods tuned for them specifically;
the comparison here is between families under a common protocol, not against the state of the
art on each problem. With three to five runs, and one on two rows, the spreads support the
large orderings above and not fine distinctions between adjacent families. And every kernel
column here inherits the tuning protocol Section~\ref{sec:retune} measures: the length scale is
chosen on a $6{,}000$-row subsample and the winner refit on every row, and this difference in sample size can affect the selected hyperparameters. On the EMIT table that costs the isotropic kernel a
factor $0.882$; on ClimSim, where the subsample is a sixteenth of the solve, re-choosing the
scale against the solve that actually runs halves it and lowers the kernel's error by $3.6\%$;
on a $20{,}000$-row corpus it changes nothing. These examples show that tuning can affect the comparison. They do not imply that every
reported kernel error is pessimistic or that a reported gap is an upper bound: validation
retuning can also increase test error. The columns of Table~\ref{tab:corpora} describe the
specified training and selection procedures, not the optimum attainable by either family.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The main EMIT comparison supports the use of both input scaling and learned features.
The input-scaled Mat\'ern kernel substantially reduces radiance error relative to the
isotropic kernel, cubic regression and width-512 network. Residual fitting improves the
network under the implemented PCA projection. The width-2000 network has a larger
standalone error under the tested optimizer and training budget, yet its features support
more accurate kernel predictions at every main split. Width and training duration change
together, so the experiment concerns fitted representations rather than an intrinsic
benefit of width.

Forward and inverse rankings nevertheless differ. The input-scaled kernel has the smallest
median reflectance error in the main comparison, while the feature kernels have smaller
95th-percentile errors. Both narrow and wide stacks increase the latter tails at every
split relative to their feature kernels. For the wide model, a mean radiance gain of
$0.0006$ percentage points accompanies an increase from $7.57$ to $22.71$ points in the
mean within-split 95th-percentile reflectance error. Neither a single forward score nor a
reflectance median summarizes this behavior. The unconditioned inverse also produces
large finite outliers, so these all-band scores should not be interpreted as retrieval
accuracy on a specified physical passband.

Theorem~\ref{thm:transfer} gives a precise explanation of why such a reversal is possible.
On its physical domain, the constrained retrieval error is at most $e_R/t$, capped at
$R$, with constant one. A distributional bound separates the small-transmission mass
from errors relative to transmission on the remaining entries. Proposition~\ref{prop:attained}
shows sharpness of the transfer exponent for this inverse under the stated population
hypotheses, not minimax optimality over all retrieval procedures. Theorem~\ref{thm:profile}
shows that a conditional root-mean-square component error proportional to transmission
is sufficient to retain the component-error rate. This is not a necessary condition in
every distribution: its other cases also allow rate retention for sufficiently light tails.
The finite transmission profiles do not establish which asymptotic regime applies to EMIT.

The distinction between fitting and scoring is equally important. The diagnostic stack
fits a convex quadratic in raw component errors, with direct and diffuse flux treated
separately, and projects the combined flux only for retrieval. Its deterministic transfer
bound has factor four. This differs from the factor-three bound for a total-flux quadratic
that includes the flux cross term. Both bounds require the physical hypotheses and
errors on the evaluation distribution; neither turns a low validation objective into a
test guarantee. The implemented equality-penalized NNLS and fixed-sweep IRLS routines
produce approximate weights. Their recorded predictions remain empirical results, not
certificates of globally optimal retrieval or forward performance.

For residual correction, the exact alignment identity and PCA decomposition identify
which error terms can improve and which remain. A learning-rate claim additionally
requires a uniform regression bound and control of the fitted residual in the relevant
source norm; the measured learning curves do not establish these conditions.
Proposition~\ref{prop:perturb} similarly controls movement between two fitted systems
without asserting generalization. Its residual-action bound can be much smaller than
the spectrum-only bound, but several reported ratios remain very large. A finite bound
is not necessarily an adequate approximation tolerance for an application.

The supplementary studies reinforce metric- and task-dependent comparisons rather than
a universally preferred family. Concatenated features improve mean radiance error on
three of four follow-up splits and improve their aggregate reflectance tail. The matched
correction-coefficient scores favor residual-corrected networks relative to the published
reference values, with the reference not retrained here. OCO-2 coefficient and radiance
losses select different trade-offs; its quadratic fitting objectives must be distinguished
from the mean relative norms used for reporting. The further-corpus results concern the
specified budgets, reductions and kernel approximations, not each corpus's best available
method.

Accuracy must also be considered alongside cost. A dense kernel matrix uses order $n^2$
storage and its factorization order $n^3$ operations. One
$18{,}884\times18{,}884$ double-precision matrix occupies about $2.85$ GB before workspaces
and other arrays. Feature kernels require both neural evaluation and a kernel prediction
against the training design; a stack evaluates every active head. No matched throughput
measurement establishes a speed advantage over operational emulators such as sRTMnet
\cite{srtmnet}.

The principal empirical limitation is the adaptively explored, fixed table. Overlapping
partitions are not independent external validation. The principal reproducibility
limitation is that public summaries do not replace raw arrays, full-precision predictions
and simulator provenance. Additional transmission summaries include nonpositive-flux and
albedo-out-of-range entries; their all-band diagnostic expressions are not certified
population bounds. A deployment evaluation should fix the application passband and
coverage requirement before comparison, separate physical failures from numerical
failures, and report both the raw and constrained inverse. Within the scope actually
tested, neural features and exact kernels complement one another, while a small forward
gain can coexist with a substantially worse inverse-error tail.

are all-band scores; supplementary common-mask and transmission diagnostics do not
constitute a validated operational retrieval protocol.
