\begin{abstract}
We compare neural networks, exact Mat\'ern kernel regression and their combinations as
emulators of a six-dimensional radiative-transfer table on the 285-band EMIT grid, and we
study how their forward accuracy carries over to reflectance retrieval. Over ten partitions
of 23{,}313 states, a kernel with one length scale per input reaches $0.095\%$ mean relative
radiance error and a network $0.387\%$; a kernel on the concatenated hidden features of five
networks reaches $0.076\%$. Forward and inverse rankings disagree. A convex stack improves on
a wide feature kernel by only $0.0006$ radiance percentage points, yet its 95th-percentile
reflectance error is larger at every partition, three times larger on average. We prove that on the physical domain
the constrained retrieval error is at most the retrieval-weighted component error divided by
the transmission, with constant one, that the rate this implies is sharp, and that errors
proportional to the transmission lose no rate. [[TQ-ABSTRACT]]
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Atmospheric correction for imaging spectroscopy relates the radiance measured at the sensor
to the reflectance of the surface through radiative-transfer calculations that depend on the
state of the atmosphere and on the viewing geometry. Learned emulators replace repeated
simulator evaluations with a fitted map from that state to the spectral quantities the
retrieval needs. Neural emulators have been built into reflectance retrieval
\cite{srtmnet,isofit}, Gaussian-process emulators have been developed for the OCO-2 forward
model \cite{lamminpaa}, and physics-guided multi-fidelity networks use a cheaper code as a
second source of information \cite{pkan}. Two questions follow: which representation gives
the most accurate forward emulator, and which of its errors matter once the emulator is
inverted.

We study exact kernel regression, fully connected networks and two post-hoc combinations of
the two. In residual correction the network supplies a mean and a kernel fits its training
residual. In feature regression the network supplies a frozen representation and a kernel
fits the target on that representation \cite{nmkc}. Both differ from deep kernel learning,
where representation and kernel are trained jointly \cite{wilson}, and from regularizing the
inner layers of a network with kernel flows \cite{yoo}. The comparison complements work on
kernel methods for operator learning \cite{batlle}, on neural operators such as DeepONet and
Fourier neural operators \cite{deeponet,fno}, and classical kernel regression \cite{rw}.

The data are 23{,}313 tabulated radiative-transfer evaluations on the 285-band EMIT grid
\cite{green}. The state has six coordinates and each output component is well described by
a few dozen principal coefficients, so an exact kernel solve on all 18{,}884 training rows is
affordable. On ten common partitions, the kernel with one length scale per input is four times
more accurate than the network on radiance, a residual kernel reduces the network's error by
a factor of almost three, and an exact kernel on the network's hidden features is about as
accurate as the input-scaled kernel. A wider network is a worse emulator but a better feature
map: from width 512 to width 2{,}000 the network's own radiance error doubles while the error
of a kernel on its features falls by a fifth. Concatenating the hidden features of five
networks and solving one kernel on them improves on the features of a single network at every
partition and gives the lowest radiance error of all the ten-partition comparisons.

The central empirical result concerns the inverse. The stack built on the wide network
improves mean radiance error over its own feature kernel by $0.0006$ percentage points, while
its mean within-split 95th-percentile reflectance error rises from $7.57$ to $22.71$ points,
and the tail is worse at all ten partitions. Small forward gains can therefore coexist with
large changes in the tail of the retrieval error. The reversal is not produced by the few
entries where the inverse is undefined: it persists on the physical domain of the retrieval,
with coverage and failure counts reported, [[TQ-INTRO]] and on a fresh partition evaluated
once under a hypothesis fixed in advance.

The analysis has three levels. Exact finite-perturbation identities relate radiance and
reflectance errors through the inverse denominator. A transfer inequality with constant one
bounds a physically constrained retrieval by the component error relative to the
transmission. Tail and conditional-error assumptions then give rates, including a sharp
worst-case exponent and conditions under which no rate is lost; the central quantity is the
profile of the conditional error against the transmission, not the error alone. On test data the
transmission-weighted term of the bound ranks nine emulators exactly as their 95th-percentile
retrieval errors, which the unweighted component error does not. For residual
correction, a Hilbert-space identity gives the exact criterion for improvement, and a
principal-component decomposition separates truncation error from the error of the kernel
step.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Radiative transfer and its surrogates.}
Relevant radiative-transfer codes include 6S \cite{vermote}, libRadtran \cite{mayer,emde}
and MODTRAN \cite{modtran}; lookup-table calculations enter atmospheric correction
\cite{isofit} and optimal estimation \cite{rodgers}. The carbon-dioxide product of
OCO-2 \cite{eldering,crisp} rests on the forward model and inverse method of \cite{connor,odell}.
Surrogates enter because the forward model is the cost. Neural emulation of longwave radiation
goes back to \cite{chevallier} and was carried into climate models by \cite{krasnopolsky}; later
work measures how accurate such emulators can be made \cite{veerman,ukkonen} and how learned
parameterizations behave when the host model is stepped forward \cite{rasp,brenowitz}. In imaging
spectroscopy and land-surface inference the same idea appears as emulator toolboxes for
radiative-transfer models \cite{rivera,verrelst}, Gaussian-process emulation for parameter
retrieval \cite{gomezdans}, and the wider use of Gaussian processes in earth observation
\cite{campsvalls}; \cite{srtmnet} places an emulator inside a reflectance retrieval and
\cite{lamminpaa} fits a cross-validated Gaussian process to the OCO-2 forward model. Replacing an
expensive simulator by a statistical model is a classical problem \cite{sacks}, as is using a
cheap model to inform an expensive one \cite{kennedyohagan,kennedycalib,perdikaris}, surveyed for
propagation and inference in \cite{peherstorfer}; the network of \cite{pkan} is a multi-fidelity
instance on the corpus rescored in Section~\ref{sec:results-benchmarks}. The measurement
requirements behind the tables used here are those of the EMIT mission \cite{green} and of the
surface-imaging missions that follow it \cite{cawse}.

\paragraph{Kernel regression.}
The estimators are classical: interpolation in a reproducing-kernel Hilbert space
\cite{aronszajn}, smoothing splines and the representer theorem
\cite{kimeldorf,wahba,representer}, ridge regularization \cite{hoerl} read as spectral filtering
of an ill-posed problem \cite{engl}, and the Mat\'ern family, whose smoothness index fixes the
differentiability of the sample paths \cite{stein,rw}. Rates under source and capacity conditions
are given in \cite{cdv,sutherland,fs,smalezhou}; the textbook accounts are
\cite{scholkopf,steinwart,esl}. Each component here is a vector of principal coefficients, so the
multi-output formulation is the relevant one \cite{micchelli,alvarez}. Kernels also serve as
solvers rather than regressors: Bayesian numerical homogenization \cite{owhadibnh},
operator-adapted wavelets \cite{owhadiscovel} and the Gaussian-process treatment of nonlinear
partial differential equations \cite{chenowhadi} use the same estimator, and cross-validating the
kernel itself gives the kernel-flow family \cite{owhadiyoo,hamziowhadi,yoo}.

\paragraph{Cost.}
An exact solve is cubic in the number of training rows, which has produced a long line of
approximations: the Nystr\"om method and its analysis \cite{nystrom,drineas,bachlowrank}, random
features \cite{rahimirecht} and their operator-learning counterpart \cite{nelsenstuart},
preconditioned solvers at large scale \cite{falkon,meanti}, inducing-point and variational
Gaussian processes \cite{quinonero,titsias,hensman}, and structured interpolation with
hardware-aware linear algebra \cite{kissgp,gpytorch}. At $n = 18{,}884$ the exact factorization is
affordable, and every full-data EMIT fit below is an exact solve.

\paragraph{Choosing and combining kernels.}
A kernel can be built rather than picked: spectral mixtures \cite{wilsonadams}, additive structure
\cite{additivegp}, compositional search over a grammar \cite{duvenaudstructure}, and
per-coordinate relevance fitted by marginal likelihood \cite{rw} or by cross-validation
\cite{owhadiyoo}. Several kernels can be combined instead of one being chosen, by semidefinite
programming \cite{lanckriet}, by the algorithms surveyed in \cite{gonen}, or by alignment with the
target Gram matrix \cite{cristianini,cortesalign}. Combining predictors rather than kernels is
older still \cite{bates-granger,wolpert,breiman}. Selection on held-out rows is classical
\cite{stonecv,gcv,arlot}; Section~\ref{sec:damped} records the finite-class guarantee it
provides when the selection sample is independent of every earlier fitting decision.

\paragraph{Networks, and the kernels they induce.}
Feedforward networks are universal approximators \cite{hornik}; those trained here use the
standard adaptive optimizer \cite{adam} with decoupled weight decay \cite{adamw} and early
stopping \cite{prechelt}; dropout \cite{dropout} is another standard regularizer. In the
infinite-width limit a network is a Gaussian process \cite{neal,leenngp,matthews} whose kernel is
the arc-cosine kernel of \cite{chosaul}, and gradient descent on a wide network follows the neural
tangent kernel \cite{ntk,arora}; both enter the kernel comparison of
Section~\ref{sec:results-representation} as heads. The limit is not the whole story, since a
finite network learns its representation \cite{rfm}, and the comparison between kernel machines
and trained networks is itself unsettled \cite{belkin}; a learned representation under a kernel is
what deep kernel learning \cite{wilson}, the Gaussian process with a neural-operator mean
\cite{mora} and the construction of \cite{nmkc} exploit. Kolmogorov--Arnold networks
\cite{kolmogorov,kan} are the architecture behind the multi-fidelity model of \cite{pkan}.
Operator learning supplies the other comparison: DeepONet \cite{deeponet}, Fourier neural
operators \cite{fno} and the general formulation \cite{kovachki}, with fair-comparison protocols
\cite{lufair}, physics-informed training \cite{pinn}, the cost-accuracy trade-off \cite{dehoop},
kernel competitors \cite{batlle}, and the benchmark collections \cite{pdebench,thewell,climsim}
from which Appendix~\ref{sec:corpora} draws.

\section{Data and protocol}\label{sec:data}

The data are $n = 23{,}313$ tabulated radiative-transfer evaluations provided by the Jet
Propulsion Laboratory as a lookup table for EMIT atmospheric correction. Each input
$x \in \mathbb{R}^6$ collects the atmospheric and viewing state: an aerosol optical depth in
$[0.0500, 1.2000]$, a surface elevation in $[-0.4997, 5.9998]$\,km, a water-vapour column in
$[0.0502, 6.9999]$\,cm, a relative azimuth in $[0.0029, 179.9947]^\circ$, a solar zenith angle
in $[0.0012, 64.9981]^\circ$, and a view zenith angle in $[150.0003, 179.9991]^\circ$ from the
downward vertical, that is, within $30^\circ$ of nadir; these are the sampled extrema, over the
ranges on which such tables are built \cite{isofit,modtran}. Each output consists of four
spectral components on the 285-point EMIT grid spanning 381--2493\,nm: path radiance $Y_1$,
direct flux $Y_2$, diffuse flux $Y_3$ and spherical albedo $Y_4$ (Figure~\ref{fig:examples}).
The quantities used downstream are the radiance at a surface reflectance $\rho$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fwd}
L(\rho) \;=\; Y_1 + \rho\,\frac{Y_2 + Y_3}{1 - \rho\, Y_4},
\end{equation}
and the reflectance recovered by inverting \eqref{eq:fwd} with predicted components at the true
radiance,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inv}
\hat\rho \;=\; \frac{L - \hat Y_1}{\hat Y_2 + \hat Y_3 + \hat Y_4 (L - \hat Y_1)},
\end{equation}
both evaluated at $\rho = 0.7$ in the main comparison. The table carries the states, the four
components and the wavelength grid; the simulator version and its configuration are not part of
it, and nothing below depends on them.

\paragraph{Protocol.}
The table is split into a test block of 2{,}331 rows and a remaining block of 20{,}982, of
which 2{,}098 rows are held out for validation and 18{,}884 are used for fitting; the split is
repeated with seeds 101--110. Standardization and principal components are fitted on the
training rows of each split, hyperparameters, checkpoints, coordinatewise selections and
combination weights are chosen on its validation rows, and every family is evaluated on its
test rows. Unless stated otherwise, tables report the mean and sample standard deviation over
the ten splits.

The model families and ablations were developed on this table with its test errors in view.
The ten splits therefore measure how the comparison varies with the partition; their test
blocks overlap, and their standard deviations are descriptive rather than confidence
intervals. Section~\ref{sec:paired-tails} reports paired differences on common splits.
Two evaluations in Section~\ref{sec:domain} were specified before they were run: an experiment
on the training targets, whose arms, seeds and scoring were recorded in a protocol before its
first fit, and a fresh partition with a single hypothesis fixed in advance and evaluated once.

\paragraph{Structure of the outputs.}
Three properties of the table shape the comparison. First, the outputs are low rank. On
standardized training outputs, $99.99\%$ of the variance of every component lies in the first
11--25 principal components and $99.9999\%$ in at most 44 (Figure~\ref{fig:structure}, left),
and the smallest correlation between adjacent bands is $0.994952$. All regressors below act on
the first 64 principal coefficients, whose unexplained training variance is below $10^{-6}$,
except the networks, which are trained on the 285 standardized bands. A small variance tail
is an aggregate statement; Section~\ref{sec:identity} states the projection error explicitly.

Second, the inputs matter unequally. Shuffling the aerosol coordinate reduces the validation
$R^2$ of a linear model by $0.89$, the solar zenith angle by $0.54$, and elevation and water
vapor by about $0.1$ each; the two remaining angles have smaller effects
(Figure~\ref{fig:structure}, right). This motivates a separate length scale for each input.

Third, low-degree surrogates are already accurate. Linear regression on the standardized
state reaches $R^2=0.83$, against $0.74$ for distance-weighted five-nearest-neighbors, and
cubic ridge regression has a mean relative component error of about two percent
(Table~\ref{tab:results}).

\paragraph{Conditioning of the inverse.}
In deep absorption bands the transmitted flux $Y_2+Y_3$ can be as small as $10^{-21}$, and at
a fully opaque band the radiance does not depend on the surface reflectance at all, so the
inverse is not identifiable there. Even the exact tabulated components do not invert exactly in
floating point: over the whole table the round trip has reflectance RMSE $7.4\times10^{-3}$, all
of it from the entries with the smallest transmissions (Section~\ref{sec:domain}). We therefore
report both the center and the tail of the reflectance error. In the all-band statistics a
non-finite inverse is assigned zero and counted; none occurs in the main comparison.

Differentiating \eqref{eq:inv} separates the components. Write $D = \hat Y_2 + \hat Y_3 +
\hat Y_4 (L - \hat Y_1)$ for its denominator. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sens}
\frac{\partial\hat\rho}{\partial \hat Y_4} = -\hat\rho^{\,2},
\qquad
\frac{\partial\hat\rho}{\partial \hat Y_2} = \frac{\partial\hat\rho}{\partial \hat Y_3}
 = -\frac{\hat\rho}{D},
\qquad
\frac{\partial\hat\rho}{\partial \hat Y_1} = -\frac{\hat Y_2 + \hat Y_3}{D^{2}} .
\end{equation}
At exact identifiable components $\hat\rho=\rho=0.7$, so the albedo derivative is $-0.49$
whatever the transmitted flux, while the path-radiance and flux derivatives grow without bound
as $D$ decreases. Errors in path radiance and flux are therefore the ones that matter near
opaque bands.

\section{Models}\label{sec:methods}

All models are trained per component and evaluated in physical units after undoing the
standardization. Unless stated otherwise they regress the 64 leading principal coefficients of
the standardized component. Every hyperparameter named below is selected on the validation
rows of the split at hand. The comparisons of Sections~\ref{sec:results}--\ref{sec:retune}
run on CPUs; most diagnostics of Sections~\ref{sec:results-transfer}--\ref{sec:results-benchmarks}
train their networks on one T4 GPU.

\paragraph{Reference family.} Cubic polynomial ridge regression \cite{hoerl} on all monomials of
degree at most three in the six standardized inputs, with the ridge weight selected from
$\{10^{-10},\allowbreak 10^{-8},\allowbreak 10^{-6},\allowbreak 10^{-4},\allowbreak 10^{-2}\}$.

\paragraph{Exact kernel ridge regression.} Three constructions, all Mat\'ern kernels \cite{stein}
on the standardized inputs, with the length scale expressed as a multiple of the median pairwise
distance of the fit set. The nugget $\gamma$ enters the system as $(K+n\gamma I)\alpha=Z$. The
subset baseline is a Mat\'ern-$5/2$ kernel fitted on a uniformly drawn subset of 4{,}000
training points, the landmark choice of a Nystr\"om approximation \cite{nystrom,drineas}, with
multiplier in $\{1,2\}$ and nugget in $\{10^{-8},10^{-6},10^{-4},10^{-2}\}$. The second is the
exact solve on every training row: smoothness $\nu\in\{3/2,5/2\}$, multiplier in
$\{0.5,1,2,4\}$ and the same nugget grid, tuned on validation from a 6{,}000-row training
subsample, with the winner refit on all 18{,}884 rows by one Cholesky factorization
\cite{golubvanloan}. The third gives each input its own length scale, an automatic relevance
determination \cite{neal,rw}: starting from the isotropic winner, a coordinate search over
per-input multipliers in $\{1/4,1/2,1,2,4\}$ runs for two sweeps scored on validation, after
which smoothness, scale and nugget are re-tuned at the metric found and the solve is refit on
every row.

\paragraph{Fully connected networks.} Three ELU hidden layers of 512 units per component,
trained on the 285 standardized bands with AdamW \cite{adam,adamw} at learning rate $10^{-3}$,
cosine decay, batch size 1024 and weight decay $10^{-6}$, for up to 150 epochs with early
stopping on validation loss \cite{prechelt} (patience 25). Five networks are trained per
component and split from different initializations; the tables report the first and the mean
of the five.

\paragraph{Kernel corrections of the neural mean.} The construction of \cite{nmkc}, with every
kernel stage an exact solve on all training rows. (i) \emph{Residual correction}: an exact
Mat\'ern regression, on the standardized inputs, of the network's training residuals in
coefficient space, added back to the network's projection into that space, with smoothness,
scale and nugget selected by the validation error of the corrected prediction; it is applied
to the single network and to the five-network mean. (ii) \emph{Kernel on features}: the same
exact regression with the input metric replaced by the network's last hidden layer,
standardized. This is the post-hoc counterpart of deep kernel learning \cite{wilson}, in which
representation and kernel are trained together, and of the kernel-flow regularization of a
network's inner layers \cite{yoo}; here the representation is fixed first and the kernel is
solved exactly on it. (iii) \emph{Per-coordinate selection}: for each of the 64 output
coefficients, the validation winner among the exact kernel, the network, the ensemble, the two
corrected predictors and the feature kernel. (iv) \emph{Convex stack}: per component, a convex
combination of the same six heads with weights fitted on the validation rows in the physical
relative metric, in the manner of stacked generalization \cite{wolpert} and stacked regression
\cite{breiman}.

\paragraph{Sample size, rank and width.} Section~\ref{sec:scaling} repeats the isotropic
exact kernel, the network, its residual correction and the feature kernel at nested training
sizes, at other output ranks and at other widths; the main table is the full-size, rank-64,
width-512 cell of that grid.

\paragraph{Polynomial and subset-kernel baselines.}
Cubic ridge regression gives $1.86\%$ mean component error and $0.760\%$ radiance error.
The 4{,}000-point Mat\'ern fit reduces these to $0.88\%$ and $0.332\%$. Its albedo error
depends strongly on the split: about $1.9\%$ on seven splits and $3.3$--$4.3\%$ on the other
three.

\paragraph{Full-data and input-scaled kernels.}
The isotropic kernel on all training rows reduces the radiance error to $0.239\%$ and the
path-radiance error to $0.08\%$, against $0.17\%$ for the subset fit. Its albedo error is
$2.3$--$2.9\%$: steadier than the subset fit, but larger than that fit on its seven best
splits. A separate length scale for each input brings the radiance error to $0.095\%$, the mean
component error to $0.63\%$ and the median reflectance error to $0.036$ percentage points. The
fitted scales reflect the weak azimuth dependence of the flux components: the relative-azimuth
length scale is sixteen to thirty-two times the others, with median factors of sixteen for
direct flux and albedo and thirty-two for diffuse flux, on every split for the flux components
and on nine of ten for albedo. The view-zenith scales are two to sixteen times longer, and the
path-radiance metric is nearly isotropic.

\paragraph{Networks and kernel corrections.}
The network has $1.22\%$ mean component error and $0.387\%$ radiance error; averaging five
initializations gives $0.96\%$ and $0.251\%$. Residual kernel regression reduces the radiance
error of the single network to $0.141\%$ and that of the ensemble to $0.123\%$. The feature
kernel has $0.61\%$ mean component error and $0.098\%$ radiance error, level with the
input-scaled kernel: it is lower on six of ten splits, though its mean is slightly higher. Its
median reflectance error, $0.054$ points against $0.036$, is larger on every split.

\paragraph{Selection and stacking.}
Both combiners use the isotropic full-data kernel, the network, its five-member ensemble,
the two residual-corrected predictors and the feature kernel; the input-scaled kernel is not
in the pool. Coordinatewise selection gives $0.193\%$ radiance error, worse than several of its
members. The convex stack gives $0.087\%$ radiance error, $0.57\%$ mean component error and
$0.048$ points of median reflectance error, about $8\%$ below the input-scaled kernel and $11\%$
below its best member, the feature kernel, on radiance. On mean component error it improves on
the input-scaled kernel at nine splits, by $0.06\pm0.05$ points, and on the feature kernel at all
ten, by $0.047\pm0.006$ points.

The residual second moments show why a combination can help. On path radiance, the residuals
of the isotropic kernel and of the five-network ensemble have correlation $0.27\pm0.09$, below
their error ratio of about $0.34$ at every split; for a quadratic loss this is the classical
condition under which a convex combination improves on its better member
\cite{bates-granger,nmkc}. The stack minimizes a mean relative norm instead, and the reflectance
is a nonlinear function of the components, so the condition is suggestive rather than
decisive for those metrics. The two residual-corrected predictors are far more strongly
correlated, about $0.88$ on path radiance and $0.99$ on albedo.

\paragraph{Spherical albedo.}
Albedo has the largest relative error for every family: its small values in the short-wave
infrared make relative errors sensitive to small absolute ones. The width-512 stack reaches
$1.94\%$. The learning curves of Section~\ref{sec:scaling} show little improvement with sample
size on this component; Section~\ref{sec:domain} tests whether the out-of-domain training
targets contribute to that plateau.

\paragraph{Tuning with every row in the solve.} The kernel families of
Section~\ref{sec:methods} choose smoothness, scale and nugget by the validation error of a solve
on a $6{,}000$-row subsample and refit the winner on all $18{,}884$ rows. Tuning with the full
solve instead lowers the isotropic kernel's radiance error from $0.239\%$ to $0.210\%$ and its
median reflectance error from $0.141$ to $0.107$ points, better on nine of ten splits, with the
ratio averaging $0.882$ and never above $1.001$. The selected scales differ: over the forty
component-split cells the full block picks a shorter length scale than the subsample in $26$,
the same in $12$ and a longer one in $2$. A smaller tuning fit prefers more smoothing.

Below $6{,}000$ rows the tuning subsample is the whole training block, so those rungs are
already fully tuned; at $8{,}000$ rows it is three quarters of the block and only at the top rung
is it a third. Applying the measured ratio to the top rung of Table~\ref{tab:curves} takes the
kernel's last step on radiance from $-0.183$ to $-0.334$, against a slope of $-0.305$ over the
five lower rungs, and on the two flux components from $-0.187$ and $-0.171$ to $-0.352$ and
$-0.358$, against $-0.437$ and $-0.468$. Most of the bend of Section~\ref{sec:scaling} is the
tuning protocol, and with full tuning the kernel's margin over the network at the full block is
$1.8$ rather than $1.6$.

Full-data tuning barely changes the input-scaled kernel: $0.096\%$ against $0.095\%$, better on
four of ten splits, ratio $1.005$. Its coordinate search already arrives at the scale the full
block selects.

\paragraph{Gradient-based metric.} The coordinate search costs a sweep over the six inputs at
five multipliers, twice, per component and split. The alternative fixes the metric before any
kernel is fitted: the multiplier of coordinate $j$ is the root-mean-square gradient of the fitted
cubic surrogate along $j$ over the training rows, raised to a power $p$, normalized to geometric
mean one and clipped to the search's range $[1/16, 4]$, so that weakly varying coordinates receive
longer length scales. It costs one cubic fit and six finite differences.

At $p=1$ the radiance error is $0.159\%$, below the isotropic kernel on every split, by a factor
$0.669$ on average, and above the searched metric on every split, by a factor $1.680$. The
dependence on $p$ is not monotone: $p=1/2$ gives $0.125\%$ and $p=1/4$ gives $0.157\%$. Choosing
$p$ like every other hyperparameter here, by the lowest mean validation error over the four
components, selects $p=1/2$ on all ten splits. At that exponent the gradient metric closes $79\%$
of the gap between the isotropic and searched kernels on radiance and $82\%$ on the median
reflectance error, for one cubic fit and a choice among three numbers.

The components prefer different exponents: $Y_2$ and $Y_3$ are best at $1/2$, the albedo at $1$
($2.18\%$ against $2.27\%$), and the path radiance at $1/4$. On path radiance the isotropic kernel
already reads $0.084\%$ and the searched metric $0.085\%$, while the proportional metric reads
$0.34\%$, four times the isotropic error. The gradient of the surrogate along a coordinate
measures how fast the map moves with it, not how much that coordinate helps predict, and on this
component the two disagree. Softening the exponent to $1/4$ brings $Y_1$ back to $0.085\%$, at the
cost of $Y_2$ and $Y_3$. Coordinate search can adjust each component separately, whereas the
gradient construction uses one exponent for all four.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

On this table, input scaling and learned features both pay. The input-scaled Mat\'ern kernel
reduces radiance error well below the isotropic kernel, the cubic and the width-512 network;
residual fitting cuts the network's error by almost a factor of three; and a kernel on learned
features matches the input-scaled kernel with one network and beats it with several. The width
experiment separates the two roles of a network: the width-2{,}000 network is the worse emulator
and the better feature map. Concatenating the features of an ensemble and solving one kernel on
them gives the lowest radiance error and the smallest reflectance tail of the ten-partition
comparisons, with one solve in place of a stack.

Forward and inverse rankings still differ. The input-scaled kernel has the smallest median
reflectance error of the main comparison, while the feature kernels have the smallest
95th-percentile errors, and both the narrow and the wide stack increase that tail at every split
relative to their feature kernels. For the wide model a mean radiance gain of $0.0006$ points
accompanies an increase from $7.57$ to $22.71$ points in the mean within-split 95th-percentile
reflectance error. Neither a single forward score nor a reflectance median describes this.
Section~\ref{sec:domain} shows that the effect is a property of the emulators: it holds on the
physical domain of the retrieval, with coverage and failures reported,
[[TQ-DISCUSSION]] and on a fresh partition read once under a hypothesis fixed beforehand, where
the stack-to-feature-kernel ratios of the tail are $6.60$ over all bands and $5.54$ on the
admissible domain.

Theorem~\ref{thm:transfer} explains why such a reversal is possible. On the physical domain the
constrained retrieval error is at most $e_R/t$, capped at $R$, with constant one, and a
distributional form separates the mass of small transmissions from the error relative to
transmission on the rest. Proposition~\ref{prop:attained} shows that the resulting exponent is
attained, and Theorem~\ref{thm:profile} that a conditional root-mean-square component error
proportional to the transmission loses no rate. What decides the retrieval tail is where the
component errors sit relative to the transmission, and a stack that lowers the average forward
error can move error into the entries with small transmission. The data agree: the
transmission-weighted middle term of the bound ranks nine heads exactly as their raw retrieval
tails (Section~\ref{sec:results-transfer}), while the unweighted component error does not.

Proposition~\ref{prop:stackweights} isolates the mechanism for stacks. Weights that minimize a
forward error move mass onto the head whose error sits where the transmission is small, since
there it costs little radiance, and the retrieval divides that error by the transmission; the
forward-optimal combination can then be worse than the retrieval-optimal one by any factor while
winning on radiance. The transmission-weighted quadratic $J_\tau$ of Section~\ref{sec:joint}
targets the retrieval directly: it bounds the mean squared constrained retrieval error for every
feasible weight vector, with factor three (four in its separable form), and in the example of the
proposition it is the retrieval error itself. Its weights still come from validation rows, and
the bound becomes a guarantee only on an independent selection sample
(Proposition~\ref{prop:selection}).

For residual correction, the alignment identity and the principal-component decomposition show
which parts of the error the correction can remove and which remain, and they explain why the
optimal output rank for the correction is lower than for the direct kernel. A learning rate for
the corrected predictor would need a uniform regression bound and control of the residual in a
source norm; the learning curves measure slopes over a finite range. Proposition~\ref{prop:perturb}
bounds the movement between two fitted systems; its residual-action form is often much smaller
than the spectral bound, though still large relative to the observed movement.

Across the further problems in the appendices the comparison stays metric- and task-dependent.
On OCO-2 the coefficient and radiance losses select different representations, on the
correction-coefficient benchmark the residual-corrected network improves on the published
reference values, and on the ten further configurations the convex stack is at or near the best
member of its pool.

Accuracy has a cost. A dense kernel matrix needs order $n^2$ storage and its factorization order
$n^3$ operations; one $18{,}884\times18{,}884$ double-precision matrix occupies $2.85$\,GB before
workspaces. A feature kernel needs a network evaluation and a kernel prediction against the
training design, and a stack evaluates every head, so a matched throughput comparison with
operational emulators such as sRTMnet \cite{srtmnet} remains to be made.

The evidence has limits that bear on its reading. The families were developed on this table and
the ten splits overlap, so the split-to-split spread describes one table rather than independent
data; the fresh partition guards against the choice of split and of hypothesis, not against the
table itself. The table is an adaptively explored lookup table, and an operational evaluation
would fix its passband and coverage in advance, separate physical from numerical failures, and
report both raw and constrained retrievals, as Section~\ref{sec:domain} does for the present
emulators. Within that scope, neural features and exact kernels complement one another, and a
small forward gain can come with a much larger inverse-error tail.

\section{Comparison with OCO-2}\label{sec:oco2}

The companion paper \cite{nmkc} applies the same construction to the OCO-2 radiative-transfer
emulation problem of Lamminp\"a\"a et al.~\cite{lamminpaa}: per spectral band (O$_2$, weak
CO$_2$, strong CO$_2$), a reduced atmospheric state of twenty or twenty-four coordinates maps to
forty reduced radiance coefficients, with errors on the standardized coefficients and on the
reconstructed radiance, under the ten-split protocol of Section~\ref{sec:data}. It gives a
comparison at a higher input dimension.

There a kernel on the state trails the network by a factor of nine, four and five on the reduced
coefficients: the isotropic Mat\'ern reaches $40.6\%$, $58.6\%$ and $43.4\%$ on the three bands
against $4.4\%$, $16.4\%$ and $8.2\%$ for the network, and residual correction adds little. The
coordinate search that gives a kernel on the EMIT state its own length scales is not the strongest
construction there either. Four learned metrics were run on the same ten splits on all three
bands: a kernel-flow metric \cite{owhadiyoo}, a Mahalanobis metric under the same flow, the
gradient outer product of the recursive feature machine \cite{rfm}, and per-input scales fitted by
empirical Bayes. The last is the best of the four on every band, taking the isotropic kernel's
$40.6\%$, $58.6\%$ and $44.3\%$ on the coefficients to $18.7\%$, $25.3\%$ and $16.9\%$, and on O$_2$
its radiance from $0.233\%$ to $0.150\%$; the two flow metrics agree within half a point of each
other at $26.9\%$, $29.8\%$ and $23.9\%$, the recursive feature machine reads $28.2\%$, $47.9\%$ and
$31.3\%$, and an additive kernel $38.2\%$, $46.9\%$ and $47.7\%$. (The isotropic baseline on strong
CO$_2$ reads $44.3\%$ in the metric experiment and $43.4\%$ in the baseline experiment; this band
has a spread of $\pm3.4$ because two of its ten splits land three points below the other eight.)
The feature kernel remains more accurate, by a margin that depends on the band: the exact Mat\'ern
regression on the network's frozen features reads $4.1\%$, $16.3\%$ and $8.1\%$ on the same splits,
so the best state metric is $4.6$ times that on O$_2$ but only $1.6$ and $2.1$ times it on the two
CO$_2$ bands. The empirical-Bayes metric is bimodal across splits on O$_2$, eight of ten landing near
$17.2\%$ and two at $24.7\%$, so its spread of $\pm3.2$ is thirty times the isotropic kernel's
$\pm0.1$. The exact Mat\'ern regression on the network's frozen features beats a ridge readout
refitted on the same features on every split, every band and every training loss, and a
per-coefficient choice among heads trained under different losses has lower mean error than the
release's own emulator under both reported metrics: it wins the reduced-coefficient comparison on
all ten splits on each band, and the radiance comparison on ten, nine and ten splits on O$_2$, weak
CO$_2$ and strong CO$_2$.

The release accompanying \cite{lamminpaa} also contains 976 retrieval records, each with an
atmospheric state, the full-physics monochromatic radiance at that state, its rank-40
reconstruction and the prediction of the release's Gaussian-process emulator. Every one of the 976
reduced states matches a test input of the release within $6.67\times10^{-16}$, mapping to 974
distinct test rows with the same nearest index in all three bands, so these records belong to the
test set; they supply a different target, the full-physics radiance, at those states rather than an
independent evaluation pool.

\section{Ten further emulation configurations}\label{sec:corpora}

Table~\ref{tab:corpora} extends the comparison to ten further configurations, each with
validation-based selection; the comparison concerns the families under a common protocol, not the
best method available for each problem.

The corpora are the advection equation with discontinuous initial data from the operator suite
of \cite{dehoop}, which is also the source of the structural-mechanics problem of \cite{nmkc};
Burgers' equation at three viscosities from PDEBench \cite{pdebench}; three one-step maps from
The Well \cite{thewell}: a two-dimensional turbulent radiative layer, an active-matter system and
a Helmholtz staircase; the ClimSim atmospheric physics emulation task \cite{climsim}; and the
paired correction-coefficient corpus of the physics-guided radiative-transfer work of \cite{pkan},
a third radiative-transfer emulation problem and the largest here at 487{,}790 rows. That corpus
is run in both of its configurations: from the atmospheric state alone, and with the low-fidelity
6S coefficients as additional inputs, which is a different and easier problem.

Advection is the 200-point input/output pair of the operator suite at full resolution. Burgers is
PDEBench's \texttt{1D\_Burgers\_Sols\_Nu}$\nu$ release, the map from the initial condition to the
solution at $t = 1$ on a grid subsampled by four to 256 cells, with the input used raw and the output
reduced to 128 principal coefficients. The three Well datasets are one-step maps of the full
multi-channel state with 256 principal coefficients in and out: the turbulent radiative layer on
its $128 \times 384$ grid (density, pressure, velocity), active matter on $256 \times 256$
(concentration, velocity and the two tensor fields), and the Helmholtz staircase on its two pressure
channels. ClimSim is the low-resolution release, 124 inputs to 128 tendencies, with 100{,}000
training rows drawn from the 10.1 million available and validation and test taken from the release's
own validation and scoring files. The correction-coefficient corpus is the paired 6S/libRadtran
Sentinel-2 set \cite{vermote,mayer}: wavelength, solar zenith, view zenith, relative azimuth,
aerosol optical depth, water vapor, ozone and elevation map to three libRadtran coefficients, split
by atmospheric state so that all bands of a state stay together; the multi-fidelity configuration
adds the three 6S coefficients, for input dimensions eight and eleven. For these two configurations
the error is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coefficient-floor}
 E_{0.05}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
 \frac{\|Y_i-\widehat Y_i\|_2}{\max\{\|Y_i\|_2,0.05\}},
\end{equation}
and the same floor enters validation selection and the kernel-flow objective, which limits the
weight of coefficient vectors of small norm.

The families are those of Section~\ref{sec:methods} with two changes forced by scale. The
reference is a ridge regression linear in the inputs, since a cubic expansion of a 256-dimensional
input is not tractable. Kernel solves are exact when the training block has at most 20{,}000 rows
and use a Nystr\"om approximation \cite{nystrom} on 6{,}000 uniformly drawn landmarks above that,
which applies to ClimSim, the Helmholtz staircase at 20{,}384 rows and the two
correction-coefficient configurations. The convex stack and the per-coordinate selection are formed
from the kernels, the networks, the residual correction and the feature kernel; the ridge is a
reference row, not one of their members. Advection, Burgers, ClimSim and the two
correction-coefficient configurations have three to five random splits. The Well's releases carry
their own training, validation and test files, which are used unchanged, so the three
turbulent-radiative-layer runs share one split and differ only in network initialization (their
spread, of order $0.01$ points, measures initialization), and the active-matter and
Helmholtz-staircase rows are single runs.

The relative errors vary widely with the problem. Where the map is smooth and the sample small,

seven, of the same kind as the five- to six-fold gap between the per-input kernel and the network on

the map rough, on ClimSim and on the correction-coefficient corpus at nearly half a million rows, the
network leads instead, $25.10\%$ against $27.20\%$ and $3.75\%$ against $62.93\%$, which is the OCO-2

other family lands within $0.4$ points of it.

Learned metrics help most on the correction-coefficient corpus. From the state alone the isotropic
kernel is at $62.93 \pm 0.51$ and a kernel-flow metric \cite{owhadiyoo} takes it to $3.97 \pm 0.42$,
a factor of sixteen, which brings a kernel on the state within six percent of the network on a
corpus where the plain kernel was seventeen times worse. With the low-fidelity coefficients as
inputs the same pair reads $15.76$ and $4.60$, a factor of three: the easier the problem is made for
the plain kernel, the less the learned metric adds.

contain. On advection the ridge is lower by $0.07$ points while the stack, at $11.36\%$, matches its
best member, the isotropic kernel, on which it puts weight $0.87$, to two decimals. On the Helmholtz
staircase the ridge reaches $0.004\%$, sixteen times below the next row and about a hundred times
below the weakest member of the pool, and the stack reaches $0.066\%$, slightly below its best member,
the learned-metric kernel at $0.067\%$, on which it puts weight $0.94$. In both cases the stack does
what it is asked within its pool and cannot recover a family the pool excludes. A combination is a
good default when no member of its pool dominates it, and it can be read against the whole
comparison only when the pool contains the families being compared.

These rows use one training budget per corpus and one architecture family, and several of the
corpora have published results from methods tuned for them. With three to five runs, and one on two
rows, the spreads support the large orderings above and not fine distinctions between adjacent
families. Every kernel column also inherits the tuning protocol of Section~\ref{sec:retune}, with
the length scale chosen on a $6{,}000$-row subsample and the winner refit on every row. On the EMIT
table that costs the isotropic kernel a factor $0.882$; on ClimSim, where the subsample is a
sixteenth of the solve, re-choosing the scale against the full solve halves it and lowers the
kernel's error by $3.6\%$; on a $20{,}000$-row corpus it changes nothing. Retuning can move a
comparison in either direction, and the columns of Table~\ref{tab:corpora} describe the stated
training and selection procedures.
